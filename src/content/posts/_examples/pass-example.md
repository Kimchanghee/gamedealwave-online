## Does the interface on this listing match what the PS5 needs?

Sony's support page says the PS5 expansion slot takes an M.2 SSD that uses **PCIe Gen4 x4 NVMe**. The example listing names "PCIe 4.0 x4, NVMe 1.4" in its title and again in the technical details table. That is the first line on the checklist, and it is the line most often missed, because SATA M.2 drives look identical in photos.

Two things to confirm on any real listing before going further:

- The words "NVMe" and "PCIe 4.0" (or "Gen4") both appear in the seller's text, not only in a third-party question.
- The model number in the title matches the model number in the details table. Listings that bundle several capacities sometimes mix model numbers across options.

If either of those is missing, the right move is to open the maker's own product page and check the model number there before you buy.

## Is the capacity and size code inside Sony's limits?

Sony lists a supported capacity range of 250GB to 8TB and accepts the 2230, 2242, 2260, 2280 and 22110 size codes. The example option is **2TB in the 2280 size**, which is the most common combination on the market and sits in the middle of both ranges.

The size code matters because the slot has a fixed screw position for each length. A 2280 drive uses the 80mm position. The console ships with the screw and spacer you need, so no extra parts are required for this size.

Capacity matters for a second reason: the usable space after formatting is always a bit lower than the label. A 2TB drive reports less than 2,000GB once the console formats it, which is normal and not a sign of a fake drive.

## Will the heatsink fit under the slot cover?

This is where the "heatsink version" option detail earns its place on the tag. Sony asks for effective heat dissipation, such as a heatsink or heat transfer sheet, and publishes maximum dimensions for drive plus cooling: the total height limit is the number to compare with the listing.

The example listing gives the heatsink height in its specifications section. A real pick only goes up when the listed height is at or below Sony's limit, or the maker states PS5 compatibility on its own page. If a listing only says "fits PS5" in its title and gives no dimensions, that claim alone is not enough to pass this line.

Some sellers offer the same drive with and without a heatsink as two options on one page. The option you select changes what arrives, so this pick names the exact option on the tag beside the article.

## Is the read speed fast enough?

Sony recommends a sequential read speed of **5,500MB/s or faster**. The example drive is rated for 7,000MB/s reads by its maker, which clears the recommendation with room to spare.

The PS5 runs its own speed test when you install a drive and shows the measured result. That number is the one that counts, and it can come in under the rated figure; the rated number on a listing is a ceiling, not a promise.

## What should you check after you buy?

Once the drive arrives, three quick checks catch most problems:

1. The model number on the drive label matches the listing you ordered.
2. The heatsink is attached the way the maker shows, with no protective film left on the thermal pad.
3. The console's format screen reports the drive and its read speed without an error.

If the console refuses the drive, the [disclaimer](/disclaimer/) explains what this site can and cannot promise, and the retailer's return window is the place to start. GameDealWave does not handle orders or returns.

## Who should skip this kind of drive?

If you only play a handful of games and delete them when you finish, the internal storage may be enough, and a USB drive can hold PS4 games and park PS5 games you are not playing right now. The [Xbox storage checklist](/guides/xbox-series-storage-checklist/) covers the different rules on Microsoft consoles, where M.2 drives are not an option at all.
